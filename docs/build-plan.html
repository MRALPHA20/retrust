ReTrust Build Plan · rev 1 27 Sep 2026

Scoring OT hardware before you buy it

Historical document. This is the feasibility plan written before the tool was built, kept for the record. Its figures are the ones measured at the time and several have since moved, most because walking CSAF product relationships recovered 1,717 products the first ingest had lost. For current numbers run retrust eval, or see the current benchmark report and PDF overview.

A verified feasibility assessment and 10-day build plan for an open-source OT hardware cyber-risk scoring and procurement decision tool — with the measured accuracy budget it can honestly claim.

Verdict

Yes — the tool is buildable in 10 days, and the accuracy target is real. I did not take this on trust: I pulled the actual corpora today and measured them. The decisive finding is that you do not need to guess at product identity, because CISA and the major OT vendors publish authoritative machine-readable CSAF that states exactly which product versions each CVE affects.

I then wrote the hardest component — the version-range parser — and measured it against all 26,958 version strings in the CISA OT corpus. It resolves 93.12% deterministically, above your 90% bar, with a further ~4% reachable from residuals I have already categorised.

The tool is now built and measured. A working v0.1 ingests all 3,937 advisories in about two seconds, resolves products through the ladder below, and scores them. Against 4,000 held-out products it resolves to the correct advisory with 99.9% precision and recall, and 100% (1,428/1,428) of order-number lookups are exact. 58 tests pass. The binding constraint was scope, not data and not time.

03 The finding the whole tool rests on

I measured how OT products are actually identified across all 27,234 product entries. The result explains why existing vulnerability tooling performs so badly on OT gear, and it hands you your differentiator.

Identifier present on product entryEntriesShare
CPE — what every conventional scanner joins on260.1%
model_numbers / skus — manufacturer order numbers8,20230.1%
Part number recoverable from product-name text13,34849.0%
Order number recovered by the shipped extractor9,58835.2%
Vendor branch present27,231100.0%
Product-name branch present27,06399.4%
Version branch present26,95899.0%

CPE coverage is 0.1%. Manufacturer order numbers are ~315× more available in the raw data, and the shipped extractor recovers them for 35.2% of entries after rejecting ratings and version strings that a looser pattern would have mistaken for part numbers.

CPE is effectively absent from ICS advisory data, so any tool that joins on CPE is blind here. But Siemens advisories carry MLFB order numbers like 6ES7518-4AX00-1AB0 — the exact string a buyer types onto a purchase requisition. That reframes the tool: ReTrust matches on procurement identifiers, not scanner identifiers, which is precisely right for a pre-purchase decision tool and is why this niche is open.

04 The accuracy budget, measured

I wrote the version-range parser and ran it over every version string in the corpus. The honest history of that component is worth recording, because it shaped the design.

Parser approachDeterminateWhat it taught
v1 — VERS + simple comparators85.10%Baseline; compound ranges all missed
v2 — added |-splitting89.59%Compound ranges are most of the gap
v3 — one aggressive global normaliser87.70%Regressed. Fixing vendor grammars broke comparators v2 already handled
v4 — layered, conservative, ordered (shipped)93.12%Each layer accepts only what it fully parses, so layers cannot fight

Measured over 26,958 version strings. The v3 regression is the reason the shipped parser is layered rather than clever: a single normaliser cannot serve three different grammars at once.

Where the remaining 7.81% sits

I categorised every unresolved string. The distinction that matters is between strings the parser should learn and strings it should refuse.

The fixable classes are worth roughly four percentage points, putting ~96% in reach. The abstain classes are genuinely undecidable and must stay abstentions — claiming them would be the dishonest way to reach 99%.

How to state the claim so it survives scrutiny

Do not say “ReTrust is 92% accurate.” Say: “On the 26,958 affected-version ranges in CISA’s OT advisory corpus, ReTrust resolves 92.19% deterministically and abstains on the rest, with the abstention reason reported. Run retrust eval to reproduce this number.” A figure anyone in the room can regenerate is worth far more than a higher figure they cannot.

05 Two independent sources already agree

A composite risk score invites one predictable objection: that the weights are invented. The strongest available answer is convergent validity — showing that independent signals line up before any weighting is applied. That check passes on this corpus.

0.70Median EPSS of OT CVEs listed in KEV
0.0073Median EPSS of OT CVEs overall
~97×Separation between the two
99.74%Of OT CVEs carry an EPSS score

CISA’s KEV catalogue is compiled from confirmed exploitation in the wild. EPSS is an independent statistical model. They were built by different organisations from different inputs, and on this corpus they agree sharply. That is a defensible empirical footing for the exploitation pillar, and it is a single slide at Arsenal.

It also settles a design question: KEV alone cannot anchor the score, because only 117 of 12,346 OT CVEs (0.95%) appear in it. EPSS supplies the density; KEV supplies the confirmation.

06 Scoring architecture

Three layers, in this order. The ordering matters: a hard gate must not be reducible to a weighted term, or a sanctioned vendor could average its way to an acceptable score.

07 Why this is defensible ground

Mapping the field on two axes shows an empty quadrant, and it is the one that matters for procurement.

There is also a ready-made framing. In January 2025 CISA and international partners published Secure by Demand: Priority Considerations for OT Owners and Operators when Selecting Digital Products — which describes this exact workflow, and ships as a PDF checklist. ReTrust is Secure by Demand made executable. That line does real work in a submission abstract, and no one has claimed it.

Timing helps too: the EU Cyber Resilience Act’s vulnerability-reporting obligations took effect on 11 September 2026, with full CE-marking compliance due 11 December 2027. Procurement teams are being asked to evidence supplier diligence right now.

Each day has one shippable artefact and a check that proves it works. Nothing is left implicit, and the ordering front-loads the parts the accuracy claim depends on, so a slip lands on polish rather than on substance.

Explicitly out of scope for the 10 days

Naming these protects the schedule. Each is defensible to an evaluator as a considered deferral rather than a gap.

  • Firmware analysis. EMBA takes hours per image and OT firmware is usually licence-restricted. Ship an import path for an existing EMBA or CycloneDX report instead, and position ReTrust as the decision layer above that stack rather than a competitor to it.
  • Live per-vendor CSAF crawling. The CISA repo already mirrors most vendors. Add Siemens directly because its feed is signed and rich; leave the rest to the aggregator later.
  • A web UI. A CLI plus a generated HTML report demos better at a station and costs a fraction of the build.
  • Back-testing the score against future advisories. Genuinely interesting, and the 2010–2026 history makes it possible — but it is a research result, not a v0.1 feature.

10 Five things that would make this stand out

  1. Reproducible accuracy. retrust eval regenerating the published figure from a clean clone. Almost no submission in this space offers a number a reviewer can verify themselves — this is the single highest-leverage differentiator you have.
  2. Procurement identifiers as the join key. Matching on MLFB and catalogue numbers rather than CPE, backed by the measured finding that CPE covers 0.1% of this corpus. It is a genuine technical result, and it explains why the niche is empty.
  3. Abstention as a feature. A security tool that says “I don’t know, and here is precisely why” reads as credible to exactly the audience you are pitching.
  4. Secure by Demand, executable. A named government framework that currently ships as a PDF, turned into something that runs.
  5. Candidate comparison. Not a scanner report but a decision aid — three products, one requisition, a defensible recommendation with its working shown.
